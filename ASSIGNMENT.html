1. The scenario, in plain terms

Blue Ocean runs payment-processing and settlement systems for property-management and hospitality clients. Card payments flow through an acquirer, the company that processes card transactions for a merchant. The acquirer streams every money-moving event for every merchant to us. We keep each merchant's ledger, and when a merchant asks for its money we pay it out through a settlement bank.

You do not need a payments background. Here is the whole domain:

Term Meaning here
Merchant A business we settle for, such as a hotel. One merchant is one stream of events.
AUTH (authorisation) A card payment is approved and the amount is put on hold. The merchant cannot spend it yet.
CAPTURE The held payment is taken. The hold is released, and the captured amount minus our fee becomes settled money.
AUTH_EXPIRY An authorisation lapses without being captured. Its hold is released.
REFUND The merchant gives money back to a customer from a capture. Settled money goes down.
CHARGEBACK A customer disputes a capture and the bank claws it back, plus a fee. This is forced: it can push a merchant's balance negative.
Reserve A per-merchant floor that must stay in the account.
PAYOUT_REQUEST The merchant asks for amount to be sent to its bank account. We approve it only if the money is there.
Platform fee Our revenue, taken on every capture and chargeback.

That is all the finance. The engineering problem: - an ordered, lossy, duplicating stream with strict per-key ordering; - exactly-once effects in a database at high throughput; - exactly-once side effects against an external API that cannot give you exactly-once itself.

2. Production scale (Part B is graded against these numbers)

Dimension Number
Merchants 60,000, Zipf-skewed. The busiest 1% of merchants generate ~40% of events.
Event volume ~450 M ledger events / month. Steady 4,000 events/s, peak 30,000 events/s (sale days, for hours).
Ledger retention 7 years online, about 3 billion rows at steady state. Regulators can ask for any merchant's balance at any instant within 7 years: it must be answered exactly, in < 200 ms.
Gateway One live session per client_id, at most 25,000 frames/s per session. You may hold up to 16 client_ids; in production a LOGON may subscribe to a chosen subset of channels. The gateway keeps replay history for 72 hours.
Settlement bank 50 requests/s per client, 2 in-flight payouts per destination account, idempotency keys remembered for 120 s, a single live OAuth token per client, maintenance windows of up to 20 minutes.
Payouts Usually ~40 k/day, but month-end brings ~1.5 M payout requests within 6 hours.
Availability Losing an event the gateway has delivered and we have credited is not acceptable. Reader failover must complete within 30 s. Paying the same payout twice is a P1 incident.

4. The ledger rules (exact)

Events arrive as DATA frames. Every event has event_id ("<merchant_id>:<seq>", globally unique), merchant_id, type, event_time (ISO-8601 UTC, strictly increasing within a channel) and currency. Amounts are integers in minor units (cents).

Per merchant you keep: - settled - held - reserve - bank_account - currency (fixed by the merchant's first MERCHANT_CONFIG)

available = settled − reserve.

Events are applied in channel sequence order:

type Fields Effect
MERCHANT_CONFIG reserve_minor, bank_account Set reserve and bank_account. The first one also fixes currency.
AUTH amount_minor held += amount. The authorisation is now open.
AUTH_EXPIRY ref (an AUTH event_id) If that auth is open: close it, held -= its amount. Otherwise reject (no effect).
CAPTURE ref (AUTH), amount_minor, fee_minor If the auth is open and amount ≤ auth amount: close the auth; held -= auth amount (the whole hold is released, even on a partial capture); settled += amount − fee; platform[currency] += fee. Otherwise reject.
REFUND ref (CAPTURE), amount_minor If the capture exists and refunded_so_far + amount ≤ captured amount: settled -= amount. Otherwise reject. Fees are not refunded.
CHARGEBACK ref (CAPTURE), amount_minor, fee_minor If the capture exists: settled -= amount + fee; platform[currency] += fee. It may go negative. Otherwise reject.
PAYOUT_REQUEST amount_minor If amount > 0 and available ≥ amount at this point in the sequence: settled -= amount and the payout is accepted. Otherwise it is rejected (insufficient_funds).
anything else — Ignore it, for forward compatibility, but it still consumes its sequence number.

Rejected events still consume their sequence number. NOTICE and GAP_FILL frames have no ledger effect.

Payout execution. An accepted payout must be executed at the bank exactly once, using:

Bank field Value
reference the event_id
account the merchant's bank_account at that point in the sequence
currency the merchant's currency
amount_minor the requested amount

A payout to a closed account (422 account_closed) is failed. Its money stays deducted (operations handles it manually), and it must never be retried.

harness/ledger_events.py → apply_event() is the reference implementation of this table. Where the prose and the code disagree, the code wins.

5. Part A: build it

5.1 Required stack

  • Language: Python 3.11+ for your services (any framework: asyncio, FastAPI, Django, …).
  • Database: PostgreSQL 15+ is the system of record.
  • Redis: required for cross-process coordination. Your correctness must not depend on Redis being durable. We will wipe it mid-run.
  • Broker: one of Kafka / Redpanda, RabbitMQ (quorum queues or streams), NATS JetStream, or Redis Streams. Justify the choice.
  • Run command: docker compose up must bring up everything, including the harness services from harness/docker-compose.harness.yml, with no manual steps. Document how to scale each service.

5.2 Required behaviour

A1. Session ownership and the protocol. - Replicas. Run ≥ 2 session-reader replicas. Exactly one may hold the gateway session at a time. - Failover. If the leader dies (kill -9), a standby must hold the session within 15 s. - A paused leader. If the leader is paused (SIGSTOP for 45 s) and then resumes, nothing it does afterwards may corrupt the ledger or the stream. Explain what fences it. - The protocol. Implement BOSP/1 correctly: - framing; - heartbeats and TEST_REQUEST; - gap detection and resend, including lost resends; - GAP_FILL; - duplicates, whatever their poss_dup flag; - resume from a checkpoint; - credit. - Credit and durability. How your credit grants relate to durability downstream is part of the design: state it.

A2. Parallel processing with ordering. - Consumers. Run ≥ 4 ledger-consumer processes (processes, not threads) that apply events in parallel while preserving per-merchant order. - What it must survive: a consumer kill -9 mid-batch, a broker restart, and a rebalance or scale-out, all without double-applying, skipping or reordering anything. - Delivery semantics. Write down the delivery semantics at every hop.

A3. The ledger, in PostgreSQL. - Rules and effects. Apply the rules in §4 with exactly-once effect, in integer minor units. - Throughput. Sustain ≥ 3,000 events/s end to end on our review machine (8 cores, 32 GB) at 2 M events with chaos on, and catch up within 60 s of the gateway finishing. - Platform fees. Every capture across every merchant touches them. Do not let that become one hot row everything serialises on, or, if you choose to, show us the numbers that make it acceptable.

A4. Payouts. - Executors. Run ≥ 2 payout-executor processes. - What they must achieve: - every accepted payout executed exactly once; - closed-account payouts failed, never retried; - nothing lost, nothing paid twice. - What they must survive: the bank's failure modes, scheduled maintenance windows longer than the idempotency-key lifetime, and executor crashes at any point. - Shared limits. The single-token OAuth session, the global rate limit and the per-account in-flight limit are shared by all executors.

A5. Read API. The contract verify.py uses:

GET /v1/merchants/{merchant_id}/balance
  → 200 {"merchant_id", "currency", "settled_minor", "held_minor", "available_minor", "last_seq"}
     last_seq = the highest channel sequence number fully processed (NOTICE / GAP_FILL included)

GET /v1/merchants/{merchant_id}/balance?as_of=2026-11-03T14:05:09.123Z
  → 200 {"merchant_id", "settled_minor", "held_minor", "available_minor"}
     the balance after every event of that merchant with event_time ≤ as_of; all zeros before the first event

GET /v1/platform/balances            → 200 {"USD": 123, "EUR": 45, ...}

GET /v1/payouts/{payout_id}          → 200 {"payout_id", "status": "pending|paid|failed|rejected", "bank_payout_id", "reason"}
                                        404 if that PAYOUT_REQUEST has not been applied yet

as_of must have a p99 latency < 50 ms over HTTP at 2 M events, and Part B must explain how that holds at 3 B rows.

A6. Observability. - Questions an operator must be able to answer: - which channels are behind and by how much; - who holds the session, and since when (epoch); - resend and gap rates; - broker lag per partition or queue; - payouts by state, including ambiguous / unknown outcome; - bank error classes. - Endpoints: /healthz and /readyz with real checks. - Logs: structured, carrying event_id / payout_id / session_epoch.

A7. Tests. - The risky logic, not getters: - the per-channel sequencing state machine (unit, deterministic: gaps, lost resend, GAP_FILL, duplicates, reconnect); - exactly-once apply under concurrent redelivery, against real PostgreSQL; - the payout outcome matrix (every row of the table in BANK-API.md); - the cross-process limiter. - Fault injection. Include a script that reproduces at least two of the faults in §5.4 against your running stack.

5.3 How we check Part A

We run harness/verify.py check after each scenario. It must print RESULT: PASS. It checks: - every channel drained; - every balance exact; - point-in-time balances; - platform fees; - every payout's status; - the bank's own record of money moved: exactly one execution per paid payout, none otherwise.

5.4 Our fault-injection schedule (published, so it is fair)

During a single 2 M-frame run, in an order and at times we choose, we will:

  1. kill -9 the session-reader leader.
  2. SIGSTOP the (new) leader for 45 s, then SIGCONT it.
  3. kill -9 one ledger consumer in the middle of a batch; add two more consumers later.
  4. Restart the broker.
  5. Restart Redis without persistence, as if after a FLUSHALL.
  6. Restart PostgreSQL (fast shutdown).
  7. kill -9 a payout executor while a payout is in flight, and again during a bank maintenance window.
  8. Raise gateway chaos: 5× the drop rate, disconnects every 20–40 s.
  9. Shrink the bank's windows: idempotency TTL 20 s, maintenance 30 s every 90 s, 10% after-commit failures.

6. Part B: DESIGN.md

Answer with numbers and mechanisms, against §2. Generic prose ("we'd add more workers", "Kafka scales horizontally") scores zero. Diagrams are welcome; walls of generated text are not. Keep it under ~8 pages.

  1. Delivery semantics, hop by hop. For each hop: - gateway → reader - reader → broker - broker → consumer - consumer → PostgreSQL - PostgreSQL → payout executor → bank

Say whether it is at-most-once, at-least-once or effectively-once, and the exact mechanism: key, constraint, offset or checkpoint, and when it is written relative to the effect. 2. Fencing. The leader stalls for 45 s while holding received-but-unpublished frames and an open producer. A standby takes over and resumes. The old leader wakes up. - Walk through, step by step, what each process does and what stops corruption, at which hop. - What is your fencing token, and who checks it? - Why is a lock with a TTL not sufficient on its own? 3. Backpressure. At a 30 k/s peak your broker slows to 10 k/s for 10 minutes. - Where does the backlog live, how big does it get, and what bounds memory in each process? - How does credit flow control participate? 4. Partitioning and skew. - Partition count, and the key, for 60 k Zipf-skewed merchants. - What happens when one merchant alone exceeds a partition's or worker's throughput? - How do you go beyond one 25 k frames/s session (16 client_ids)? How are channels assigned, and how is that reassigned on failure without two sessions owning a channel? 5. PostgreSQL at 3 B rows: - schema, partitioning strategy and keys; - the index that serves an exact as_of in < 200 ms at 3 B rows, and the write cost it adds; - update-heavy rows (running balances, platform fees): HOT updates, vacuum, bloat, contention at 30 k events/s; - estimated WAL volume per day; - synchronous_commit and fsync choices, and why; - your connection-pooling mode, and what it forbids you from using. 6. Isolation levels. State the isolation level of every transaction, and why. Give one concrete anomaly your design would suffer at READ COMMITTED if you removed one specific lock or constraint you rely on: name it, and show the interleaving. 7. Payout maths and the ambiguity protocol. - Month-end: 1.5 M payouts in 6 h against 50 req/s and 2-in-flight per account. What is the drain time, what is the backlog, and which merchants wait longest? - Your ambiguous-outcome protocol as a state machine. - Where, under your design, could a double payment still occur, and what would detect it? 8. Failure modes. For each, what happens, what self-heals, and what pages a human: - Redis lost; - PostgreSQL failover to an async replica (what is lost; how do you detect and repair the ledger and the session checkpoint?); - a broker partition-leader loss; - a gateway checkpoint older than the 72 h replay window; - a poison event. 9. Three tradeoffs you made: what you gave up, and what you would change with more time.

7. Part C: REVIEW.md, the incumbent

incumbent/ is the previous team's version of this system. They used Kafka.

It ran in production for two weeks and was rolled back after an incident: - 14 payouts were executed twice; - 3 merchants' balances drifted from the acquirer's statements; - a further 9 accepted payouts were never sent.

The post-mortem never found the root causes. incumbent/README.md has the context.

Deliver REVIEW.md:

  1. Every defect you find, ranked by severity. For each: - file:line; - the concrete trigger: the interleaving, failure or input that makes it go wrong; - the impact: money, ordering, loss, availability or performance; - the fix.
  2. Root causes. Which defects, in combination, explain each of the three incident symptoms? Be specific: name the defects and the sequence of events.
  3. Reproducers for your top 5. An executable reproducer for each of your five most severe defects: a test or script that fails against the incumbent logic and passes against your fix. - You may extract the incumbent logic into testable units. - You may run against the PostgreSQL/Redis in your compose. - A description of a reproducer is not a reproducer.

We know of more than 20 defects. Some are the kind any linter or assistant flags in seconds; those are worth little. The ones that caused the incident are not.
